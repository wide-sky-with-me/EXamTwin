# Q19

Single-use plastic consumption creates environmental costs such as litter, marine damage and cleanup
costs borne by third parties. Consumers consider marginal private benefit (MPB), while marginal social
benefit (MSB) is lower once the external consumption cost is included. With supply representing MSC,
the free-market equilibrium is where MPB=MSC at (Q_m). The social optimum is where MSB=MSC at the
lower quantity (Q_s). A correctly labelled diagram would show MPB above MSB, the same upward MSC
curve, (Q_m>Q_s), and the welfare-loss triangle between the curves over the excess output.

# Q20

Demand for delivered food is price inelastic when the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller
than the percentage change in price. Delivery may take a small share of income in the USA and South
Korea, so a price rise has little income effect. Consumers may see delivery as a convenience necessity
because of long working hours, remote work or habits formed during the pandemic. Apps reduce search
and transaction costs and loyalty schemes increase switching costs. Close substitutes exist—cooking,
collection and eating out—but time constraints can make them weak substitutes. Inelasticity will be
strongest for higher-income or time-poor consumers and weaker when fees become large or consumers have
time to adjust.

# Q21

Governments can charge an indirect tax or levy on single-use plastic. This raises private cost toward
social cost, increases price and reduces quantity demanded; revenue can fund recycling. Its effect is
larger when demand is elastic, but an inelastic product may require a large tax and burden low-income
consumers.

A ban or production restriction can reduce waste quickly and gives firms certainty, but enforcement is
costly and substitutes may also create environmental damage. Deposit-return schemes reward collection
and internalise part of the disposal cost, though administration and return infrastructure cost money.
Subsidies for reusable packaging, recycling technology and information campaigns can shift supply or
demand without simply penalising consumers, but they have an opportunity cost and may subsidise
activity that would have happened anyway.

The best policy is a mix: a predictable levy, targeted bans where substitutes are practical, deposits,
and investment in recycling. This conclusion is intentionally underdeveloped and does not compare
international cooperation or government failure in enough depth (E04).
